# Traditional and Mobile Computing

*Chapter 1 · Part 7: Computing Environments.*

The last part of the chapter steps back and looks at the **places** operating systems run, from a desktop PC to a data center. First, the two you use every day.

## Traditional computing

Traditional computing meant **stand-alone, general-purpose machines**: one computer on one desk.

That line has **blurred**, because **most systems now interconnect with others** (the Internet, for example):

- **Portals** provide **web access to internal systems**: an employee logs in to a company's systems through a web page.
- **Network computers** (**thin clients**) are like **web terminals**: a simple machine whose real work happens on servers.
- **Mobile computers** interconnect via **wireless networks**.
- Networking is now **ubiquitous**: even **home systems use firewalls** to protect home computers from Internet attacks.

## Mobile computing

**Mobile** means **handheld smartphones, tablets**, and similar devices.

What's the functional difference between them and a "traditional" laptop? Mostly **extra features**: mobile OSes support hardware a laptop usually lacks, like **GPS** and a **gyroscope**. Those allow **new types of apps**, like **augmented reality**, which overlays information on the camera's view of the world.

For connectivity, mobile devices use **IEEE 802.11 wireless** (Wi-Fi) or **cellular data networks**.

The two leading mobile operating systems are **Apple iOS** and **Google Android**.

| | Traditional | Mobile |
|---|---|---|
| Devices | Desktops, laptops | Smartphones, tablets |
| Extra hardware the OS supports | — | GPS, gyroscope |
| Connectivity | Wired or wireless networks | IEEE 802.11 or cellular data |
| Leading OSes | (many) | Apple iOS, Google Android |
