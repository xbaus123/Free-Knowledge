# Open-Source Operating Systems

*Chapter 1 · Part 7: Computing Environments.*

The chapter ends with a different kind of question: not how an OS works, but **who gets to see how it works**.

## Open vs closed source

**Open-source** operating systems are **made available in source-code format** rather than just as **binary closed-source** (compiled code you can run but not read or change).

That's **counter to the copy protection and Digital Rights Management (DRM) movement**, which tries to restrict what people can do with the software and media they have.

## Where it started

The movement was **started by the Free Software Foundation (FSF)**, which has the **"copyleft" GNU Public License (GPL)**.

**Copyleft** turns copyright around: anyone may use, study, change, and share GPL software, but **if you distribute a changed version, you must share your source code under the same license**. The freedom is passed along.

## Examples

- **GNU/Linux**: the Linux kernel plus the GNU project's tools.
- **BSD UNIX**, which is also the **core of Mac OS X** (macOS).
- **Many more.**

## Try them yourself

You can explore other OSes without wiping your computer. Use a **VMM** like **VMware Player** (free on Windows) or **VirtualBox** (open source and free on many platforms) to **run guest operating systems for exploration**. That's virtualization from two lessons ago, put to work for learning.

Studying open-source OSes is one of the best ways to learn: you can read the code of a real kernel, change it, rebuild it, and see what happens. Every idea in this chapter, from interrupt handlers to bitmaps, is in there.
